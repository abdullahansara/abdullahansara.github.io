<p>Human beings now occupy a place in history that has scarcely been occupied before. One aspect of this condition has appeared in earlier forms, but another seems almost without precedent. It has become possible only under the pressures of late capitalism, and through particular accounts of reality, of what human beings are meant to do, and of what they are presumed to be made of. The prior element that has appeared before in other cultures is a certain form of religiosity. It is a religiosity that imagines many domains of life as needing strict restriction, and many forms of worldly reality as inherently corrupting, if not evil. For such a mind, even causal reasoning may appear suspect, since to ask too confidently why something is so can seem like an attempt to reach beyond the limits set by God, or to place reason where divine ineffability ought to remain. This form of religiosity has existed in many traditions, and in the Muslim world, for many historical, social, and theological reasons, it still persists. It is therefore unsurprising that many Muslims experience deep anxiety around music, whether in the act of listening to it or in the very question of how one ought to approach it, since it belongs to one of those restricting domains for this peculiar religiosity. The question is rarely encountered as a simple aesthetic question or a cultivative inquiry. It arrives already mediated by inherited juridical anxieties, by particular conceptions of sin and discipline, and by a moral imagination in which sound itself is a site of suspicion. Whatever may be said in defence of or against this behaviour, one fact remains difficult to deny. It belongs, unmistakably, to a habit of mind that is pre-modern as well.</p><p>The more striking condition is the second one, which has not existed as thoroughly, at least in the Muslim world. This is the production of music that has, in effect, stripped itself of the deeper capacities of expression while retaining some quasi-form musicality. It evokes the appearance of aesthetic seriousness without necessarily possessing its substance. There is, of course, some value, particularly for the teenager, in engaging with lyrics that are immediate, predictable, and emotionally cliché. Yet the greatest literature has never been merely immediate and surface merely in this manner. Its greatness lies in its depth, its many-sidedness, and its power to sustain more than one intention at once, essentially its “Soul”. This is why we return to Hafez, Rumi, Ghalib, Shakespeare, Dante, Goethe, Milton, Cervantes, Blake, Wordsworth, Eliot, and Dostoevsky. Their works do not exhaust themselves upon first encounter. Or at second. Or at third… They deepen with us. In one sense, this loss of Soul may be worse than the first condition. In the first condition, the absence is clear. Music is denied, restricted, or treated with suspicion. But in the second condition, even the absence is absent. One remains under the illusion that music still exists, when in truth only its shell may remain. If by music we mean not merely organised sound, but the disciplined expression of inward life, then some music cannot be called music proper.</p><p>This condition has become possible only through the particular nature of our political economy, our spiritual outlook, and our prevailing conceptions of existence. It cannot, of course, be overturned by individual will alone. Yet the individual learner is not entirely helpless before it. One may still resist its effects by entering deeply into a classical and traditional discipline, preferably one in which musical form and sung expression remain joined together. Such a discipline trains attention, refines taste, and restores to sound something of its older inward seriousness. Most importantly, it does for the listener what may be called, in one sense, the final cause of all music. It opens the spirit. It restores the listener, however briefly, to a state in which sound is a passage toward inward ecstasy.</p><p>In India, because of its extraordinarily rich spiritual inheritance, many musical traditions have preserved this older spirit more fully than much of the modern Western world. Among them, perhaps the most famous Muslim tradition is <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qawwali">Qawwali</a>. Qawwali shows us that Islamic philosophy, in one form at least, was never confined entirely to the learned elite. It entered the lives of ordinary people through sound, poetry, rhythm, and devotion. Anyone may experience such music, but the music itself is filled with spiritual allusions whose deepest meanings become visible only within an Islamic philosophical and mystical worldview. In this sense, Qawwali was not and is not merely a popular art. It is a vehicle through which metaphysical and mystical longings are made available to the many. One need look no further than the celebrated Qawwali singer <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fareed_Ayaz">Farid Ayaz</a> to see how polymathic learning and the older ideal of the unity of knowledge shape not only the music itself, but also the very culture of listening around it.</p><figure><img alt="Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, the celebrated Pakistani Qawwali vocalist, shown in a contemplative pose that evokes the devotional interiority of Indo-Muslim musical performance. Such music is made to be tasted. It is ordered toward that primordial expression of divinity which prophecy receives in its most perfect form. In Sufi mystical thought, particularly in its South Asian expression, mindful listening is not merely an aesthetic act. It may become a form of worship. Indeed, it may become among the highest forms of worship when it produces in the seeker a moment of witnessing, an inward experience of presence. The words often used for such moments are state (<em>kayfiyyat</em>) and finding (<em>wajd</em>). These states are meant to be the final cause of the musical experience. What the Prophet, by the perfection of his soul, was able to receive directly, the seeker may approach only by participation, reflection, and projection. Music becomes one such means. It gives the listener, in however partial and diminished a form, a passage toward the kind of inward opening that prophetic experience discloses in its fullness. Although many examples of this kind of music could be offered, I shall confine myself to one well-known instance, partly because it is widely available with subtitles and may therefore be approached by a broader audience.</p><div class="video"><iframe allowfullscreen="" loading="lazy" src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/cj7roem9NRc?rel=0&amp;autoplay=0&amp;showinfo=0&amp;enablejsapi=0" title="Embedded video"></iframe></div><p></p><p>Several features allow this music to be situated within a traditional and mystical setting. First, and perhaps most importantly, there is the self-understanding of the musicians themselves. They do not approach their music merely as performance or entertainment. They understand it in devotional and mystical terms, as a service to humanity and to God, by allowing listeners to taste something of that great ecstasy which lies at the heart of sacred music. Then there is the use of language. Many compositions draw upon Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Hindavi, and Punjabi, creating a multilingual beauty in which several Islamicate literary worlds coexist. In many cases, even Hindu mystical themes enter the musical imagination, not as distant foreign and exotic intrusions, but as part of the shared devotional vocabulary of South Asia. The result is a form of sacred music in which language, both form and words, work together to form a mystical essence.</p><p>Consider, for example, how the themes of the Unity of Being (<em>waḥdat al-wujūd</em>) and mystical longing are woven into the piece cited above. The music does not present metaphysics as an abstract doctrine. It allows metaphysics to become audible through music. The lover, the beloved, the act of longing, and the divine presence toward which longing moves are gathered into a single devotional atmosphere, where poetry, melody, and repetition train the listener to perceive unity beneath apparent multiplicity. This is precisely why such performances are long and repetitive, a feature that often frustrates those who seek from music only the simplest and most immediate pleasures. Let us now comment on the lyrics:</p><p style="text-align: right;">مالكُ الملكِ لا شريكَ له، وحدَه لا إلهَ إلا هو</p><p><em>Māliku l-mulki lā sharīka lahu, waḥdahu lā ilāha illā huwa. (Arabic)</em></p><p>The Possessor of sovereignty, with no partner unto Him. <br/>He alone, there is no god but He.</p><p style="text-align: right;">شمسِ تبریز، گر خدا طلبی<br/>خوش بخوان لا إلهَ إلا هو</p><p><em>Shams-i Tabrīz, gar khudā talabī,<br/>khush bikhwān lā ilāha illā huwa.</em></p><p>Shams of Tabriz, if you seek God, <br/>recite sweetly: there is no god but He.</p><p style="text-align: right;">کونین کا مسجود ہے، معبود ہے تو<br/>ہر شے تیری شاہد ہے، مشہود ہے تو</p><p><em>Kawnayn kā masjūd hai, maʿbūd hai tū;<br/>har shay terī shāhid hai, mashhūd hai tū.</em></p><p>You are the one before whom both worlds bow. You are the worshipped one.<br/>Every thing bears witness to You. You are the one beheld.</p><p style="text-align: right;">ہر ایک کے لب پر ہے تیری حمد و ثنا<br/>ہر سوز میں، ہر ساز میں، موجود ہے تو</p><p><em>Har ek ke lab par hai terī ḥamd-o-sanā;<br/>har soz meṅ, har sāz meṅ, maujūd hai tū.</em></p><p>On every lip is Your praise and glorification.<br/>In every burning sorrow, in every melody, You are present.</p><p style="text-align: right;">تیرے ہی نام سے ہر ابتدا ہے تیرے ہی نام تک ہر انتہا ہے<br/>تیری حمد و ثنا، الحمد للہ کہ تو میرے محمدؐ کا خدا ہے</p><p><em>Teray hī nām say har ibtidā hay; teray hī nām tak har intihā hay. <br/>Terī ḥamd-o-sanā, alḥamdulillāh; keh tū meray Muḥammad ka khudā hay.</em></p><p>With Your name alone does every beginning start. <br/>Until Your name alone does every ending reach. <br/>Your praise and glorification, ‘all praise belongs to God’, <br/>For You are the God of my Muhammad.</p><p>Even the most cursory reading reveals that the passage is speaking of divine unity in its deepest and most uncompromising sense. God is not being described as one existent among other existents, nor as a ‘supreme maximal being’ placed at the summit of a hierarchy of beings. He is presented as the one Reality whose essence is identical with Existence itself. All that appears to exist other than Him has no independent standing of its own. It is like the wave before the sea, the ray before the sun, or the image in the mirror before the face that gives it form. The wave has no being apart from the ocean, the ray has no light apart from the sun, and the image has no reality apart from the one reflected. The song actually uses such analogies later in the song. </p><p>The passage begins with a conventional formula of monotheistic affirmation, one that would be acceptable to all Muslims, yet it gradually carries that formula into a more explicitly Sufi register. It points toward the intuition that the multiplicity of the world is a veil drawn across the face of unity. The world appears before us as a garden of countless colors, but every color receives its being from a single light. Things are traces of His self-disclosure, shadows cast by His presence, and signs through which the Real makes Himself known.</p><p>This music, though sung publicly and received by ordinary listeners, contains a depth that could be unfolded with the same philosophical density one finds in Ibn Sīnā’s metaphysics, Ibn ʿArabī’s prose, Ādi Śaṅkarācārya’s non-dual insight, or even the austere structure of Spinoza’s Ethics. That is precisely its beauty. What remains hidden in the most difficult works of philosophy is made audible here in the simplest form, so that everyone may taste it without first passing through the discipline of technical language. In our tradition, metaphysics is not confined to the page. It is, in a very real sense, everywhere. That is what true metaphysics entails. It becomes breath, rhythm, repetition, longing, emotion, love, and cry. The singer does not argue for the Real. What need is there for an argument when the Real is brought forth to be experienced? When They circle around the listener until they begin to feel what argument alone cannot disclose. A single repeated name, a rising melodic phrase, or a trembling invocation can perform the work of an entire philosophical treatise. If one questions it, they need to listen until they do not. This music can make dependence, unity, annihilation, and divine presence felt before they are even slightly conceptually understood.</p><p>Will such a tradition endure for long? Perhaps not. Will humanity continue to invest itself in forms that demand time, surrender, apprenticeship, and inward refinement, especially when they stand against the dominant ethics of productivity and consumption? Likely not. The present political economy will, in time, attempt to absorb even this, just like it has done to many things. Yet while we are here, in this last interval before everything is flattened, we may still enjoy the beauty of this music for one more day. We may still listen for that opening which arrives suddenly in the <em>wajd</em>-inducing repetitions of Nusrat, or in the aching cries of Munshi Sahib. And perhaps, in one of those moments, the veil will thin for an instant, and what the philosophers sought through demonstration will be granted to the listener through sound, a <em>burhān</em>, nevertheless.</p>
