<p>Islamic philosophy, as a field within the global, and particularly Western, academy, is of fairly recent formation. Even as late as the 1960s, one could scarcely expect many Western scholars, with a few distinguished exceptions such as Henry Corbin, to explain the nature of Islamic philosophical argument or Avicenna, in general, with the same intimacy and competence that Western Thomists brought to Thomas Aquinas, or Aristotelians to Aristotle. The field has certainly changed over the past few decades. We now possess a far richer body of scholarship, and a growing number of scholars have devoted themselves to its texts, figures, and complications. Yet there remain vast provinces of the tradition still only dimly illuminated by the academic lantern.</p><p>One such neglected question is of constructive philosophy. The Christian intellectual world in the West has long enjoyed the privilege of having its inherited traditions not merely described, but also philosophically extended, presented, and argued for. Thomism, Augustinianism, and various forms of Christian metaphysics have been allowed to argue, respond, revise themselves, and live again in contemporary thought, not merely as historical artefacts, but as living systems capable of illuminating the human condition. Islamic philosophy, by contrast, has too often been placed under glass, like an important but fragile artefact whose chief duty is to be admired without being used. If one wishes to present Islamic philosophy in the academy, one is usually expected to do so in a descriptive manner. One may explain what al-Fārābī said, what Ibn Sīnā meant, what Suhrawardī proposed, or what Mullā Ṣadrā attempted. But to think with them, to extend their arguments, to ask what their metaphysics might contribute to contemporary philosophy, is still treated as a somewhat suspicious enterprise, as though the poor Muslim philosopher had arrived at the conference without the proper European credentials. With the very recent involvement of confessionally Muslim scholars in constructive and analytic philosophy, this situation has begun to improve, and we may expect to see more of such work in the future. This is a topic to which we shall return in a later episode. For the present article, however, our concern lies elsewhere.</p><p>In addition to the limitations on the constructive front, Islamic philosophy has also suffered in its historical treatment. We know a fair amount about the tradition in Iran, and somewhat less, though still substantially, about parts of the Arab world. This is partly because philosophical and <em>ʿirfānī</em> learning has remained visibly alive, and at least partially flourishing, in places such as Qum and its surrounding seminaries. By contrast, the philosophical traditions of Africa, India, and Southeast Asia have scarcely received the attention they deserve. To write a complete history of Islamic philosophy, one must also examine its development within these traditions and geographical regions.</p><p>Among them, India clearly occupies a place of special importance, not only because of the scale and longevity of Muslim intellectual life on the subcontinent, but also because it became a meeting ground for Arabic scholasticism (mainly from the South), Persianate metaphysics, Sufi speculation, and the older philosophical traditions of India itself. The decline of Persian among the learned classes of the Subcontinent has ensured that many works remain understudied, unpublished, and, one fears, quietly fading away for want of proper archival care. To alter this situation, we must return to these works themselves, edit them with care, and subsequently publish them. Even among the texts that are available, hardly any have received the sustained attention they deserve in the writing of any serious intellectual history, let alone in the construction of a broader account of Islamic philosophy in the Subcontinent.</p><p>In this blog, which I have dedicated to Shaykh al-Maqtūl, Yaḥyā b. Ḥabash al-Suhrawardī (d. 587/1191), and aptly named <em><strong>Naẓar-i Ishrāqī</strong></em> after his Illuminationist school, <em>Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq</em>, I hope to open a modest window onto this neglected world. My concern is not merely to catalogue forgotten names, though even that would be a service, but to ask how Islamic philosophy lived, argued, and transformed itself in the intellectual landscapes of India and beyond. Suhrawardī is conventionally identified as the founder of the Illuminationist school and is associated with the title Shaykh al-Ishrāq (the Master of Illumination). Those inspired by him and his school left their mark not only on Islamic philosophy in India, but also on other intellectual worlds, for he was among the first thinkers to present a serious synthesis of mysticism and discursive philosophy.</p><p>This very question of the relation between reason and mysticism is, I believe, one of the central dividing lines in any account of philosophy in India. On the one hand, we find a form of religiosity deeply shaped by folk traditions. In this sense, Islamic philosophy is not confined to manuscripts and madrasas. It is all around us, appearing in shrine culture, talismans, devotional practices, and the symbolic imagination of ordinary religious life. Although parts of this world have come under attack through the curious alliance of modernity, reformism, and fundamentalism, they remain woven into the fabric of Muslim religiosity in the Subcontinent. By this definition, the philosophical tradition is not dead at all. It is very much alive, though perhaps not always in forms approved by the sober academic or even a traditionalist scholar.</p><p>Alongside this stands the school of non-rational mysticism, which constitutes another modality on the same broad continuum. For this school, Ibn ʿArabī (d. 638/1240) remains a major figure, as does the wider discipline of theoretical mysticism. Yet its interest lies less in discursive reasoning or the detailed architecture of the purely rationalist tradition and more in the mystical experience itself. This current, too, has received some attention, though probably still less than folk practice and popular devotional culture.</p><p>What has received hardly any attention is the discourse surrounding the rationalist philosophy of Islam, inspired by masters such as al-Fārābī (d. 339/950), Ibn Sīnā (d. 428/1037), and Suhrawardī. This tradition, too, admits of further subdivisions, especially between those thinkers who joined rational inquiry to mystical speculation and those whose concerns remained more exclusively rationalist. A serious research question, therefore, remains. Did India produce a figure of the stature of Mullā Ṣadrā (d. 1050/1640), one capable of speaking with equal authority on the questions of philosophy and mysticism? To answer this question, we must attend not only to original works of Islamic philosophy, but also to the commentaries, glosses, supercommentaries, and superglosses through which the tradition so often preserved, refined, and occasionally concealed its most subtle arguments. To read and engage with these works, one needs expertise not only in the relevant languages and discourses but also the sharp physical eye required to determine what the text says in the first place. One might devote an entire life to this field and still leave with the melancholy suspicion that one has only disturbed the dust on the first shelf. As a small preliminary effort, I have added a <a href="https://x.com/ShaykhIshraq/status/2060569682654970133?s=20">brief bibliography</a> of relevant sources on my X account, which I hope soon to expand into a fuller article here. To this, one might also add the following list of scholars who belonged, in one way or another, to this broader tradition.</p><p>The list is plainly non-exhaustive. It is offered not as a map of the whole territory of Islamic philosophy in India, but as a few lamps placed along the road. Many names are missing, many connections remain to be drawn, and many works still await careful editing, publication, translation, and interpretation. Yet even such an incomplete list may serve a useful purpose. It reminds us that Islamic philosophy in India was not a minor provincial echo that may be ignored. It was  a dense and living intellectual world, sustained by teachers, commentators, glossators, jurists, mystics, logicians, and metaphysicians. With this modest gesture, I end the present article. The purpose has not been to settle the question, but to open it.
